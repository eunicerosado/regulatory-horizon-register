## From 1 February 2027 cryptoasset exchange and custodian wallet providers must apply enhanced due diligence, senior management approval and a shell bank prohibition to third-country correspondent relationships, under new regulation 34A of the Money Laundering Regulations

**HMT · UK · legislation** · Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Amendment) Regulations 2026, S.I. 2026/621, regulation 20 inserting regulation 34A into S.I. 2017/692

Materiality: **High** (4.6) · actionability 5/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-06-09 · effective 2027-02-01 · compliance date 2027-02-01

*Who it catches:* crypto asset service providers, credit institutions  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, compliance, onboarding, legal, board

Regulation 20 of the Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Amendment) Regulations 2026 inserts a new regulation 34A into the Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017. Regulation 1(4) of the amending instrument provides that regulation 20, and regulation 36(b) which adds regulation 34A to Schedule 6, come into force on 1 February 2027. Regulation 34A applies where a cryptoasset exchange provider or custodian wallet provider acts as correspondent to a respondent providing similar services from a third country. In addition to the regulation 33 measures, the correspondent must gather information about the respondent, check its reputation and the quality of its supervision, assess its AML/CFT controls, document each party's responsibilities, and obtain senior management approval before establishing a new correspondent relationship. It must also be satisfied that the respondent has verified and monitors customers with direct access to the correspondent's accounts and can provide information on request. Regulation 34A(2) prohibits entering into or continuing a correspondent relationship with a shell bank, and 34A(3) requires enhanced measures to avoid relationships with credit or financial institutions known to permit shell banks to use their accounts. The score is 5 on actionability because this is a binding obligation with a date fixed in the instrument, and it requires a control that does not currently exist in most firms: correspondent due diligence has been a banking discipline, and the population now subject to it has largely never built it. Reach is 4 rather than 5 because the obligation attaches to a defined licence category, with credit institutions caught only where they sit in such a relationship. The shell bank prohibition in 34A(2) is the provision with the sharpest consequence, because it bites on continuing relationships and not only new ones, so a relationship already on the book must be assessed rather than grandfathered. Recorded out of window deliberately: the instrument is not new, but the 1 February 2027 date was carried only in the 12 October edition's prose and belongs in the calendar. The instrument was made on 9 June 2026, as stated in its header and signature block on the made instrument page on legislation.gov.uk, read on 2026-10-13; `published` records that date. The 1 February 2027 date is stated expressly in regulation 1(4). The default commencement in regulation 1(2), 21 days after the day the Regulations were made, governs the instrument's other provisions and is not this record's obligation. Continues the cryptoasset thread at 2026-10-06-uk-001.

**Action:** Cryptoasset exchange providers and custodian wallet providers: identify every third-country correspondent relationship, including those already on the book, and build the regulation 34A file for each — respondent information, reputation and supervision checks, an AML/CFT control assessment, a written division of responsibilities, and evidenced senior management approval. Establish who holds the senior management approval mandate before 1 February 2027 rather than after. Screen the respondent population for shell banks and for institutions permitting shell bank access, and plan exits for relationships that cannot be continued. Credit institutions in such relationships: confirm which side of the correspondent-respondent relationship the firm sits on, because the obligation follows the correspondent role and not the licence.

## OFAC adds the International Criminal Court to the SDN List and issues four general licences covering transactions with the Court, telecommunications and enterprise software, pension payments and certain detainees

**OFAC · US · sanctions action** · OFAC recent actions 9 October 2026; ICC-EO14203; ICC-related General Licences 13, 14, 15 and 16

Materiality: **High** (4.6) · actionability 5/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-10-09 · effective 2026-10-09

*Who it catches:* credit institutions, payment service providers, all regulated firms  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, operations, compliance, legal

OFAC added the International Criminal Court to the SDN List on 9 October 2026, recorded as INTERNATIONAL CRIMINAL COURT, Oude Waalsdorperweg 10, 2597 AK, The Hague, Netherlands, established 01 Jul 2002, tagged [ICC-EO14203]. It issued four ICC-related general licences the same day: GL 13 authorising certain transactions involving the Court, GL 14 authorising certain transactions related to telecommunications and enterprise software, GL 15 authorising transactions related to pension payments, and GL 16 authorising certain transactions related to certain ICC detainees. Scored 5 on actionability on the same basis as every other same-day designation record in this register: a listing has immediate effect, list ingestion and screening must run, and exposure must be blocked or worked to a licence. Reach is 4, consistent with 2026-08-23-us-002, 2026-08-25-us-001, 2026-08-30-us-002 and 2026-09-14-us-003. What distinguishes this designation operationally is that the designated party is an international organisation rather than a company or an individual, so the exposure sits in categories most sanctions programmes do not model: payroll and pension flows, telecommunications and software supply, professional and legal services, and detainee-related payments. Those are precisely the four categories the general licences address, which means the licences define the whole of what remains permitted and firms are working licence-by-licence rather than policy-by-policy. The practical risk is not a missed screening hit on the name itself, which is unmistakable, but authorised-but-unidentified flows sitting in vendor, payroll and benefits systems that no sanctions screen reads. The same action day also carried a Burma-related designation removal, and a separate release the same day issued a Russia-related general licence; both are list maintenance and are not recorded separately.

**Action:** Ingest the designation and screen the customer, counterparty, vendor, payroll and benefits populations for exposure to the Court, not only the payment screening population. Read GL 13, 14, 15 and 16 and map each identified flow to a specific licence or block it; record which licence each permitted flow relies on and its conditions, because outside those four authorisations nothing is permitted. Brief legal and HR as well as sanctions operations, since pension, telecommunications, software and professional-services exposure will not surface through transaction screening alone.

## From 25 October 2027 a substituted Schedule 6B governs changes in control of registered cryptoasset businesses, taking effect for all remaining purposes on the day the UK cryptoasset regime commences

**HMT · UK · legislation** · Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Amendment) Regulations 2026, S.I. 2026/621, regulation 37 substituting Schedule 6B to S.I. 2017/692

Materiality: **High** (4.0) · actionability 4/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-06-09 · effective 2027-10-25 · application date 2027-10-25

*Who it catches:* crypto asset service providers  
*Whose desk:* legal, compliance, board

Regulation 37 of the amending instrument substitutes Schedule 6B to the Money Laundering Regulations 2017, which governs changes in control of registered cryptoasset businesses. Regulation 1(5) splits its commencement: paragraphs 1, 2 and 5 of the new Schedule take effect 21 days after the Regulations are made, and the substitution takes effect for all other purposes on 25 October 2027. Recorded separately from 2026-10-12-uk-001 because it carries a different obligation, a different date and a different set of affected functions. The date is the point worth holding: 25 October 2027 is also the day the UK cryptoasset regime commences and the date already recorded at 2026-10-06-uk-001, so a firm going through the FCA authorisation gateway and a corporate transaction in the same period will meet both on the same day. Actionability is scored 4 rather than 5 because the obligation is binding with a dated application but is contained to the legal and corporate governance function rather than requiring systems or monitoring change, which is the one-function limb of level 4 in rubric.md; the dated application awaiting commencement also fits the limb added on 2026-10-06. Reach is 4 because it attaches to a defined licence category. The partial commencement is the trap: part of the new Schedule is already live and part is not, so a change-in-control question asked in 2027 has two possible answers depending on which paragraph it turns on. The instrument was made on 9 June 2026, read on the made instrument page on 2026-10-13, so the 21-day limb fell 21 days after that date, at the end of June 2026, and is already in force. Continues the thread at 2026-10-06-uk-001.

**Action:** Registered cryptoasset businesses and their advisers: read the substituted Schedule 6B and establish which paragraphs are already in force and which arrive on 25 October 2027, before relying on either position in a transaction. Where a change in control is contemplated across that date, confirm which notification regime applies and build the timetable around 25 October 2027 rather than discovering it.

## FinCEN withdraws both outstanding digital asset proposals — the recordkeeping and reporting rule for convertible virtual currency transactions including unhosted wallets, and the special measure treating CVC mixing as a class of primary money laundering concern

**FinCEN · US · rule withdrawn** · Federal Register documents 2026-20430 and 2026-20429, both filed 5 October 2026 and scheduled for publication 6 October 2026

Materiality: **Medium** (3.2) · actionability 2/5 · reach 5/5

Published 2026-10-05

*Who it catches:* crypto asset service providers, money service businesses, credit institutions  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, compliance, technology, board

FinCEN announced on 5 October 2026 that it is withdrawing two proposed rules on convertible virtual currency. The first would have imposed recordkeeping, verification and reporting requirements on certain CVC transactions, including those involving unhosted wallets, withdrawn by Federal Register document 2026-20430. The second was the proposed special measure treating CVC mixing as a class of transactions of primary money laundering concern, withdrawn by document 2026-20429. Both withdrawal notices were filed with the Federal Register on 5 October 2026 and scheduled for publication on 6 October 2026; the mixing withdrawal was read on the Federal Register public inspection page, which gives a filing time of 08:45 on 5 October. FinCEN said it considered the public comments submitted on both proposals and framed the withdrawals as part of the administration's deregulatory agenda and its effort to make digital asset rules fit-for-purpose. No official was quoted on the release and the original proposal dates, dockets and RINs were not stated there, so none is recorded. Actionability is 2: nothing binds and nothing becomes unlawful, but a planning assumption moves, and that is the level 2 reading. Reach is 5 because the withdrawn proposals would have reached covered institutions across banking, money services and virtual asset activity rather than one licence category. Two consequences. First, firms that declined mixer-exposed deposits or built mixing-attribution rulesets have partly been relying on the direction of travel, and that support is gone; the position now has to be written down as the firm's own risk appetite with a named owner, rather than attributed to a pending rule. Second, the unhosted-wallet withdrawal leaves the self-custody boundary open in the US while the EU transfer-of-funds regime and the FATF standard treat it as closed, so a cross-border firm cannot operate a single rule set. Read against 2026-10-06-us-001, the pattern is a change of instrument rather than retreat: the A7 Network special measure proposal published 5 October is still live, so class-based rules are giving way to named-target measures, which moves the burden from building a control once to absorbing designations continuously. Instrument type is recorded as rule_withdrawn, added to taxonomy.md on 2026-10-13 for this record: the artefacts are Federal Register documents withdrawing proposed rules, and recording them as rule_proposed would have misstated the direction of travel, which is the point of the item. Continues the thread at 2026-10-06-us-001.

**Action:** Firms with crypto exposure: restate the firm's own position on mixer-exposed flow and on the self-custody boundary as a documented risk appetite with a named owner, rather than as anticipation of a US rule. Where a global rule set was being designed to the withdrawn proposals, re-baseline it on the EU transfer-of-funds regime and the FATF standard, which have not moved. Confirm the sanctions-screening and designation ingestion capability can absorb named-target special measures at pace, because that is the instrument now in use.

Source: <https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-announces-withdrawals-proposed-digital-asset-related-rules> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## DOJ National Fraud Enforcement Division issues Directive 26-13, requiring prosecutors to charge fraud across tax, securities, consumer fraud and cybercrime authorities and to use pre-indictment asset seizures and digital-infrastructure takedowns

**DOJ · US · supervisory statement** · Directive 26-13; DOJ press release 26-1160

Materiality: **Medium** (3.2) · actionability 2/5 · reach 5/5

Published 2026-10-08

*Who it catches:* credit institutions, payment service providers, money service businesses, crypto asset service providers  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, operations, legal, compliance

The Justice Department published Directive 26-13 on 8 October 2026, issued by Assistant Attorney General Colin M. McDonald of the National Fraud Enforcement Division and setting out the Division's prosecutorial methodology; the release is numbered 26-1160 and was updated 9 October 2026. It requires prosecutors to charge fraud using tax, securities fraud, consumer fraud and cybercrime authorities together, which the release calls a significant operational shift, and to use early, pre-indictment asset seizures and digital-infrastructure takedowns to halt schemes and freeze assets in real time. It also directs prosecutors to develop cooperators, to prosecute corporate wrongdoers, to seek arrest and detention in appropriate cases, and to advocate thoroughly at sentencing. The release cites a Government Accountability Office estimate that the federal government loses between $233 billion and $521 billion a year to fraud. Stated priorities are to prosecute fraud whatever its size or complexity, to preempt and dismantle networks before they inflict widespread harm, and to restore public confidence in federal spending. The full directive is linked from the release; the release does not describe self-disclosure, monitor or declination policy, and nothing is recorded here about those. Actionability is 2: no obligation attaches to any firm, so this is direction of travel, but the planning assumption that moves is operational rather than legal. Reach is 5 because the effect runs across US financial institutions generally. The consequence worth recording: pre-indictment seizure means legal process arrives earlier and with less context, as a seizure warrant or restraint on an account whose customer has not been charged and is not publicly linked to anything. That is a workflow question before it is a legal one — who decides whether to exit, what the SAR says, what the customer is told, and how the firm avoids tipping off while also avoiding an unexplained freeze it cannot justify to a complaints function. Multi-authority charging compounds it, because one customer's conduct now generates overlapping requests from tax, securities and cyber angles, typically answered by three teams inconsistently. Digital-infrastructure takedowns have a detection consequence: when infrastructure is seized mid-scheme, inbound mule flow stops abruptly rather than tapering, which is observable for a firm looking for it. Instrument type is recorded as supervisory_statement, the house answer for a DOJ statement of its own approach, consistent with 2026-08-25-us-003. DOJ is a prosecuting authority rather than a supervisor, so the label is a fit by function — an authority's published statement of how it will act — rather than by form; per the non-equivalence note in taxonomy.md the distinction is stated here rather than forced into a new value.

**Action:** US institutions: name the owner of the legal-process workflow end to end — receipt, validation, account restraint, customer communication, SAR decision and exit decision — and test it against a restraint on an uncharged customer rather than a post-indictment one. Confirm that overlapping requests about one customer from different authorities are answered from one record rather than by three teams separately. Flag abrupt cessation of inbound mule flow as a monitoring signal rather than a resolved alert.

Source: <https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/assistant-attorney-general-colin-m-mcdonald-issues-memorandum-national-fraud-enforcement-1> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## FCA publishes survey findings on corporate finance firms' compliance arrangements, naming the governance artefacts firms lack and committing to engage further with selected firms on the adequacy of compliance resources

**FCA · UK · report** · FCA survey of corporate finance firms' compliance functions, flagged in the Regulatory Priorities Report

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-10-08

*Who it catches:* investment firms  
*Whose desk:* compliance, board, legal, risk

The FCA surveyed 411 corporate finance firms and received 382 responses, publishing the findings on 8 October 2026 under the name of Jamie Whitehorn, Head of Trading Venues and Corporate Finance Firms; the page records 6 October 2026 as first publication. The population is small: 64% have nine or fewer employees and 89% fewer than 50. Sixty-three per cent have a compliance function carrying other business responsibilities, which the FCA notes can create conflicts where compliance helps generate revenue, and 90% use some form of third-party compliance support. Forty-seven per cent carry out all the activities surveyed. Of those that do not: 140 firms do not include regulatory compliance in staff performance assessments, 90 have no whistleblowing arrangements, 82 have no breach register, 82 have no compliance attendance at management body meetings, 82 produce no annual compliance report to the management body, 75 have no compliance sign-off for certain business actions, and 74 do no compliance monitoring of complaints handling. The FCA said compliance should be a culture embedded by senior managers rather than "simply a series of tasks", that senior management remains responsible where third parties provide support and firms must "stay actively involved", and that for the 32 firms in the sample holding public markets roles — AIM nominated adviser, AQSE corporate adviser, listing sponsor — "strong and credible challenge is essential". Scored 3 on actionability because this is a supervisory expectation rather than a rule change, and firms are expected to be able to evidence a position; the FCA states it will engage further with selected firms on the adequacy and effectiveness of compliance resources, which is what gives the expectation teeth. Reach is 3 because the surveyed population is a business-model sub-population rather than a whole licence category, though the read-across to any small authorised firm where the compliance officer also holds a commercial role is the more useful part. The structural point: every gap the FCA names is an evidentiary artefact rather than a control — a register, a report, a minute, a sign-off, a performance objective — and publishing the base rates tells a firm both what will be asked and how unusual it is to be missing it. The conflicts finding is the harder one, because in a nine-person firm proportionality has been carrying the argument, and the FCA's position is that proportionate is acceptable only where it still delivers high standards of conduct and effective conflicts management, which is a judgment about outcomes answered with records. Instrument type is recorded as report: the artefact is a blog post, but its content is survey findings with stated expectations and a supervisory follow-up, which is what the UK column of report describes as a thematic review.

**Action:** Corporate finance firms, and small authorised firms generally: check whether each artefact the FCA counted exists and is current — breach register, annual compliance report to the management body, compliance attendance at that body, whistleblowing arrangements, compliance sign-off, compliance monitoring of complaints handling, and regulatory compliance in staff performance objectives. Where the compliance function also carries commercial responsibilities, document how the conflict is managed rather than asserting that it is. Where third-party compliance support is used, evidence the firm's own active involvement and the senior manager who owns the output.

Source: <https://www.fca.org.uk/news/blogs/strong-compliance-functions-support-trusted-corporate-finance-markets> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## OFAC settles with freight forwarder Pegasus Worldwide Logistics for $175,015 over ten apparent violations, six of which were for failing to respond fully to an OFAC subpoena over six months

**OFAC · US · enforcement** · OFAC settlement with Pegasus Worldwide Logistics, recent actions 9 October 2026

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-10-09

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms, credit institutions  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, operations, legal, compliance

OFAC announced a $175,015 settlement with Pegasus Worldwide Logistics on 9 October 2026, resolving potential civil liability for ten apparent violations. Four were apparent violations of the Illicit Drug Trafficking Sanctions Regulations: between September 2023 and February 2024 the company, a freight forwarder, allegedly dealt in blocked property by facilitating the importation of a machine supplied by a designated company. The other six were apparent violations of the Reporting, Procedures and Penalties Regulations, for failing to respond fully to an OFAC subpoena over a six-month period. OFAC determined the conduct was non-egregious and not voluntarily disclosed. Scored 3 on actionability, consistent with the enforcement records at 2026-09-07-uk-001 and 2026-09-14-us-004, because the transferable point is a supervisory expectation firms should be able to evidence a position on rather than a rule change. Reach is 3: the respondent is one firm in a niche activity, but the count distribution generalises to any firm that receives administrative process. The reason to record a small settlement: most sanctions programmes are built around the first four violations — screen the counterparty, catch the designated supplier, do not move blocked goods — and almost none are built around the other six. A subpoena response is a project: locating records across systems, reconstructing a transaction chain two years old, and producing it in usable form to a deadline. It typically falls between a legal function treating it as discovery and an operations function treating it as someone else's, with no owner and no budget. OFAC has priced that failure separately and, by count, more heavily than the underlying conduct. Thematically this sits with 2026-09-28-us-002, where the SEC priced the handling of its own examination process rather than only the underlying control gap; the pattern across both is that a regulator's own process is becoming a scored control.

**Action:** Name the owner of an OFAC, OFSI or other administrative information request end to end, with a documented process covering record location across systems, completeness review and deadline management. Test whether the firm could produce a complete record of a two-year-old transaction or shipment within 30 days. Firms underwriting logistics, freight forwarding and customs broking clients: note that the blocked property exposure in this case arose through facilitating an importation rather than through a payment.

Source: <https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20261009> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## PRA consults until 7 February 2027 on indexing 128 regulatory thresholds to nominal UK GDP, with the first automatic update on 1 July 2031 and five-yearly updates thereafter

**PRA · UK · consultation paper** · CP13/26

Materiality: **Medium** (2.8) · actionability 2/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-10-07 · consultation close 2027-02-07

*Who it catches:* credit institutions, insurers  
*Whose desk:* compliance, finance, risk, board

The Bank of England announced on 7 October 2026 that the PRA proposes indexing 128 regulatory thresholds for banks, insurers and credit unions to nominal UK GDP, replacing ad hoc manual updates. The thresholds determine which rules apply to a firm, how they apply and what the firm must report. The largest is the £320 billion total assets threshold for detailed capital reporting; the smallest is a £7,500 threshold covering amounts an individual owes a credit union. Others named are the size of an insurer subject to Solvency UK and the Small Domestic Deposit Takers total assets threshold. The first automatic update would be 1 July 2031, with updates every five years thereafter. The consultation opened 7 October 2026 and closes 7 February 2027. Thresholds jointly owned with the FCA are excluded and covered in a parallel discussion paper described on the page as Section 4 of CP13/26. Nothing binds yet, which fixes actionability at 2, consistent with the proposed-rule scoring at 2026-10-06-us-001 and 2026-10-06-us-003. Reach is scored 4 rather than 5 because, although the instrument covers banking, insurance and credit unions, the firms materially affected are those sitting near a threshold; recording 5 would overstate it. Recorded despite sitting at the edge of the register's financial-crime lane, because thresholds determine which reporting regimes apply and the 7 February 2027 date is a real consultation close. Two consequences worth holding. For a firm growing into a threshold, the line now moves away from it as nominal GDP rises, so a crossing planned for a given year may not happen and a programme built around it needs re-planning. For everyone else, a fixed threshold was an event that forced a firm to re-read which rules applied, and indexation removes the event — by 2031 nobody will remember that the number moved, so which regime a firm is in becomes a question with a drifting answer and no diarised moment to ask it. The cost falls on whoever maintains the regulatory inventory.

**Action:** Banks, building societies, credit unions and insurers within reach of a named threshold: identify which of the 128 thresholds the firm sits close to, and re-test any plan built around crossing one on a fixed date. Respond by 7 February 2027 where indexation changes the planning assumption. Separately, name the owner of the regulatory inventory and the review that re-checks threshold applicability, because from 2031 no threshold change will announce itself.

Source: <https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/news/2026/october/regulatory-thresholds-set-to-shift-to-automatic-increases> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## AMLA consults until 3 November 2026, via a public hearing that day, on draft RTS establishing a central AML/CFT database, including the materiality threshold above which a supervisor must report a breach

**AMLA · EU · consultation paper** · AMLA draft RTS establishing a central AML/CFT database

Materiality: **Medium** (2.8) · actionability 2/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-10-07 · consultation close 2026-11-03

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms, designated non financial businesses  
*Whose desk:* compliance, financial crime, data, legal

AMLA opened a consultation on 7 October 2026 on draft regulatory technical standards establishing a central AML/CFT database. The database brings together data on individual obliged entities and on supervisors and their activities, and becomes the single point for supervisory authorities in the EU to report information, which authorities can also retrieve. The RTS set out how and when supervisors report, the scope and level of detail required, a materiality threshold above which a breach must be reported, and the conditions under which AMLA may request further information, with a staged entry into force in some cases to give supervisors time to adapt. AMLA states the database draws on data supervisors already hold or will collect anyway and adds no new reporting costs for obliged entities. The consultation runs via a public hearing on 3 November 2026, 14:00 to 16:00 CET, with a 16:00 CET deadline that day; the deadline was read on AMLA's public consultations page, which lists this as the only open consultation. Nothing binds an obliged entity, which fixes actionability at 2 and is consistent with the AMLA roundtable record at 2026-10-06-eu-003. Reach is 4, matching that record, because the database covers the whole obliged-entity population but the direct reporting duty sits with supervisors. The statement that there is no new cost for obliged entities is accurate and beside the point. The consequence is that a supervisory finding stops being local: today a breach recorded by one national supervisor reaches another through a call, a college meeting, or not at all, which is why a group can be in a difficult conversation in one member state and a routine one in another. A central record with a defined materiality threshold means any EU supervisor can read a group's history before speaking to it, and inconsistency between what a group tells different supervisors becomes visible by default rather than by accident. The materiality threshold is the whole design — set low, every finding travels; set high, only crises are recorded — and it is being settled by supervisors talking to supervisors unless obliged entities respond. Instrument type is recorded as consultation_paper: the artefact is a consultation on draft RTS, so the final instrument will be a technical_standard, but what is published now is the call for input.

**Action:** Groups operating in more than one member state, and any firm mid-way through a remediation plan agreed with one national supervisor: read the draft RTS and respond on the materiality threshold and the scope and level of detail of supervisor reporting by 3 November 2026, 16:00 CET. Separately and regardless of whether the firm responds, reconcile what has been told to each national supervisor about the same facts, because the database makes divergence legible. Identify who owns the record of open supervisory findings across the group.

Source: <https://www.amla.europa.eu/press-release-amla-consults-draft-standards-eu-wide-amlcft-database_en> · re-checked 2026-10-12

## AMLA asks the Commission to bring staking, lending and borrowing into scope, define effective control over DeFi arrangements and review why certain asset-referenced token issuers are not obliged entities, in its MiCA review response

**AMLA · EU · report** · AMLA response to the European Commission targeted consultation on the review of MiCA

Materiality: **Medium** (2.8) · actionability 2/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-10-07

*Who it catches:* crypto asset service providers  
*Whose desk:* compliance, legal, financial crime, risk

AMLA published its response to the Commission's targeted consultation on the MiCA review on 7 October 2026, assessing the framework from an AML/CFT perspective. It raises five areas: bringing staking, lending and borrowing within the regulatory framework given their specific ML/TF risks; a clear legal definition of DeFi arrangements with common criteria for determining who exercises effective control; a consistent EU-wide approach to unauthorised stablecoins to remove legal uncertainty; a review of the interaction by which certain asset-referenced token issuers are not treated as obliged entities; and clearer information requirements to support cross-border supervision of service providers. AMLA states it stands ready to support the Commission in its review. Nothing binds, which fixes actionability at 2, matching the ESMA MiCA response at 2026-10-06-eu-002 and the EBA priorities at 2026-09-28-eu-004. The reason to record it is that it completes a pattern rather than adding a view: the EBA asked about definitions and multi-issuer stablecoin reserves, ESMA asked for powers to freeze assets and block websites, and AMLA has now asked about the obliged-entity perimeter itself. Three authorities approaching the same consultation from prudential, markets and AML mandates have each concluded that MiCA regulates the wrong set of activities, which is a stronger planning signal than any one of the three submissions. Two limbs carry disproportionate weight. A defined test for effective control over a DeFi arrangement will be applied to structures documented specifically to establish that nobody exercises control, and those documents were drafted against no definition. And becoming an obliged entity is not a tuning exercise for an asset-referenced token issuer currently outside the population: it is a business-wide risk assessment, a CDD programme, monitoring, an MLRO and a reporting line into a national FIU, none of which exists today. Instrument type is recorded as report, matching the treatment of the ESMA response at 2026-10-06-eu-002; the artefact is a consultation response rather than a consultation, and report is the closest fit in taxonomy.md. Continues the thread at 2026-10-06-eu-002 and 2026-09-28-eu-004.

**Action:** No action required. Planning assumptions to move: do not build a staking, lending or borrowing product on the assumption it stays outside the AML/CFT perimeter; re-read any structure paper asserting that no person exercises effective control over a DeFi arrangement, on the assumption a definition will be applied to it; and asset-referenced token issuers currently outside the obliged-entity population should size what an AML/CFT programme would cost to stand up rather than discovering it on adoption.

Source: <https://www.amla.europa.eu/amla-identifies-amlcft-priorities-review-mica_en> · re-checked 2026-10-12
