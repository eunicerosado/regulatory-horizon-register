## On 13 November OFAC hit two Guatemalan trafficking groups

**OFAC · US · sanctions action** · Treasury press release sb0689; OFAC recent actions 20261113

Materiality: **High** (4.0) · actionability 4/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-11-13 · effective 2026-11-13

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms, credit institutions, money service businesses  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, compliance, onboarding, legal

Los Mendoza DTO was designated under Executive Order 14059 and under Executive Order 13224 as amended by Executive Order 13886 for material support to the Sinaloa Cartel; Los Huistas DTO was designated under Executive Order 13224 as amended for material support to the Sinaloa Cartel and CJNG, having been designated under Executive Order 14059 on 18 March 2022. Concurrently the State Department designated Los Lorenzanas as a Foreign Terrorist Organization under section 219 of the Immigration and Nationality Act and as an SDGT. The counter-terrorism authority is what changes the risk: exposure moves from a civil blocking question to material support, and Treasury states that significant transactions by foreign financial institutions risk restrictions on US correspondent or payable-through accounts. Actionability is 4 rather than 5 because the obligation is immediate but contained to screening and relationship review with no dated implementation step. Reach is 4 rather than 5 because the live counterparty exposure is concentrated in the Guatemala, Honduras, Belize and Mexico corridors, even though the blocking duty itself binds every US person. Continues the thread at 2026-08-23-us-002, whose 20 August action designated an Ecuador-based trafficking network under Executive Order 14059 with several members also under Executive Order 13224.

**Action:** Re-run screening and 50 percent rule ownership aggregation across the book for the newly designated organisations, and separately review commercial counterparties in the named corridors for material support exposure under the counter-terrorism authority, which the standard sanctions file is not written to answer.

## On 12 November DOJ moved to seize $1.2bn of DPRK crypto

**DOJ · US · enforcement** · Justice Department press release No. 26-1356

Materiality: **Medium** (3.4) · actionability 3/5 · reach 4/5

Published 2026-11-12

*Who it catches:* crypto asset service providers, credit institutions, payment service providers  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, compliance, technology, operations

The Department obtained court authorisation to seize or restrain more than 1.2 billion dollars of assets stolen from Binance on or about 29 October, a theft the FBI attributes to the North Korean group it calls TraderTraitor and which the release puts at more than 6 billion dollars; collective government and private sector efforts have frozen more than 2.7 billion. The same release covers seizure of infrastructure behind a social engineering scheme the FBI calls FaceStealer, more than 400 million dollars over 18 months. The second-order point is the recovery ratio itself, which is now a public figure a firm can use when sizing a control, and the laundering route the release names, bridges, mixers and exchanges, which puts the recoverable window in hours. Actionability is 3 and not 1 because the reasoning generalises: the blocking expectation reached firms through a cyber alert to virtual asset service providers rather than through a list update, which is a routing problem most control environments have not solved. Reach is 4 rather than 5 because the obligation lands on virtual asset service providers and their banks rather than across every sector. Continues the thread at 2026-11-02-us-002, the OFAC designations of a DPRK IT worker network whose listings carried digital currency addresses.

**Action:** Confirm who in the firm receives law enforcement cyber alerts and how an address-level blocking expectation in one reaches the screening or wallet-screening engine, with a recorded time from receipt to live control. Record the recovery ratio for this event in the crypto theft risk assessment rather than a vendor detection claim.

## On 13 November the FBI warned on AI-run intrusion tooling

**DOJ · US · advisory** · FBI FLASH FLASH-20261113-001, Threat Overview of ARTEX Autonomous Penetration Testing Framework

Materiality: **Medium** (3.2) · actionability 2/5 · reach 5/5

Published 2026-11-13

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms  
*Whose desk:* technology, risk, operations

The FLASH describes ARTEX, an open-source framework using large language models for autonomous penetration testing, adapted by malicious actors to automate intrusion from reconnaissance through data theft, with some intrusions reportedly reaching privileged access in under three hours. The original repository was removed in October 2026 but forks, mirrors and a commercial variant persist, and researchers counted more than 1,600 publicly exposed consoles in early November 2026. Financial services is first on the list of targeted sectors. The mitigations are conventional: inventory internet-facing applications, restrict management interfaces, least privilege on multi-tenant permissions, monitor privileged role changes, rate-limit high-impact APIs, alert on bulk record access, and preserve sign-in and audit evidence before irreversible changes. The consequence that is not conventional is governance: incident response and notification plans assume a human attacker's reconnaissance period, and known gaps carried on a remediation plan were priced against that assumption. Actionability is 2 because no obligation attaches and the proper response is a change in planning assumptions. Reach is 5 because ten sectors are named. board is not listed in functions_affected because the FLASH requires no governing-body decision, attestation or documented oversight; re-presenting an accepted gap is this register's recommendation, not an obligation.

**Action:** Re-test the timing assumptions in the incident response plan, escalation matrix and third-party notification clauses against an intrusion reaching privileged access in hours, and re-present any accepted gap whose risk acceptance relied on a longer detection window.

Source: <https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2026/261113.pdf> · re-checked 2026-11-16

## On 13 November Johnson Controls paid $5m over cyber terms

**DOJ · US · enforcement** · False Claims Act settlement; United States ex rel. Vance v. Johnson Controls, Inc., No. 1:24-cv-2500 (N.D. Ga.); United States ex rel. Thomas v. Johnson Controls Int'l PLC, No. 1:26-cv-448 (D.D.C.)

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-11-13

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms  
*Whose desk:* risk, compliance, technology, legal

Johnson Controls, Inc. and two subsidiaries agreed to pay 5 million dollars to resolve allegations that from January 2018 to August 2025 they failed to comply with cybersecurity requirements in federal contracts, including failing to control access to protected information, failing to maintain a System Security Plan meeting contract requirements where NIST SP 800-171 applied, and failing to adequately document, develop or implement a plan of action and milestones. Two whistleblower suits were resolved and one relator receives 850,000 dollars; the claims are allegations only with no determination of liability. The transferable lesson is the attestation surface: a contractual security representation converts an ordinary control gap into a payment claim, and the same structure sits under supplier self-attestations and certifications in third-party risk programmes. Actionability is 3 on the generalisable-enforcement reading of the rubric. Reach is 3 because the directly affected population is contractors subject to NIST SP 800-171 rather than a whole sector, even though the reasoning reaches any firm that signs or relies on a security attestation. `entity_scope: all_regulated_firms` is the least-wrong value; the real population is government contractors and outsourced service providers.

**Action:** Identify every contractual security or resilience representation the firm has signed, name an owner for each, and test whether that owner could evidence the statement under challenge today rather than at the next renewal.

Source: <https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/hvac-supplier-johnson-controls-inc-and-two-subsidiaries-agree-pay-5-million-settle> · re-checked 2026-11-16

## On 13 November the NCA settled two UWOs worth GBP 55m

**NCA · UK · enforcement** · Unexplained Wealth Order settlements approved by the High Court on 12 November 2026

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-11-13

*Who it catches:* credit institutions, designated non financial businesses  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, onboarding, compliance, legal

Two Unexplained Wealth Order cases, both linked to one of Singapore's largest money laundering cases, settled rather than litigated, with the High Court approving on 12 November. The first surrenders 75 percent of assets worth 35,187,285 pounds, from an earlier freeze of about 45 million pounds in April and July 2025 over alleged proceeds of illegal gambling and overseas scam centres, including two London properties, 2,607,700 pounds of rental income and 300,000 pounds in a bank account. In the second, 16 newbuild flats in a Soho development were bought for 24.7 million pounds in 2023 without mortgages through two UK companies incorporated for the purpose with one ultimate beneficial owner, and after an unanswered UWO in June 2026 the NCA will recover 19,771,175 pounds. The NCA says it has seized about 75 million pounds from the same group in 12 months. The generalisable lesson sits with the gatekeepers the structure passed through, company formation, conveyancing, letting and the accounts that moved the funds, and the pattern is not subtle. The second-order consequence is that settlement leaves the evidential standard for this kind of wealth untested in open court, so firms calibrate source-of-wealth standards from settlements rather than from judgments.

**Action:** Run a lookback for the pattern rather than the names: multiple unmortgaged residential purchases in one development, through companies incorporated shortly before completion, with a single beneficial owner and rental income routed back to the same party.

## On 12 November the ICO wrote to 200 health sector leaders

**ICO · UK · supervisory statement** · ICO warning to the UK health sector on unauthorised access to medical records

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-11-12 · effective 2027-02-12 · compliance date 2027-02-12

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms  
*Whose desk:* compliance, data, risk, board

The ICO reports over 300 reports of unauthorised access from health organisations between 1 January and mid-October 2026, including staff viewing records without a legitimate need and sharing information with others, and has written to more than 200 senior health leaders asking them to personally confirm that access is granted, reviewed and removed appropriately, that suspicious access is spotted through effective monitoring and audit, that staff understand the consequences, and that the organisation acts promptly when something goes wrong. Leaders should confirm they have reviewed their approach within the past six months; those who have not must complete a review within the next three months. The ICO can prosecute individuals under section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 and has prosecuted 16 healthcare staff in a decade, which is why the pressure is being applied to leadership instead. The second-order consequence is evidential: once an executive has personally confirmed that monitoring is effective, the next incident is a question about that confirmation. Dates: the three-month instruction is on the page and the publication date is 12 November 2026, so 12 February 2027 is arithmetic rather than a date the ICO printed; `effective` and `deadline` are set to the same date per the forward-dated obligation convention. `entity_scope: all_regulated_firms` is the least-wrong value; the addressed population is UK health organisations, and the reasoning reaches any organisation holding sensitive records. `board` is used because the confirmation is asked of named senior leaders.

**Action:** Confirm whether a review of records-access controls has been completed in the past six months; if not, schedule and complete one within three months of 12 November 2026, and retain evidence that access-monitoring output was read and escalated, not merely generated.

## On 10 November a fake GBP 154m takeover bid drew a sentence

**FCA · UK · enforcement** · Sentencing at Southwark Crown Court, 10 November 2026; guilty pleas entered 10 September 2026

Materiality: **Medium** (3.0) · actionability 3/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-11-10

*Who it catches:* investment firms  
*Whose desk:* compliance, financial crime, operations

A defendant was sentenced to two years suspended for two years, with 200 hours of unpaid work and 4,627.73 pounds of costs, plus three concurrent 14-month custodial sentences for forgery, after pleading guilty to fraud by false representation and three counts of making a false instrument. He had built a stake in an AIM and Toronto-listed oil and gas company, then launched a bogus bid using invented identities, forged takeover documents and a meeting with the chief executive under a false name, standing to gain up to about 70,000 pounds. The transferable point is detection rather than conduct: the FCA says investigators combined transaction reporting data, phone records, receipts, CCTV and seized evidence, linking three phone numbers to a single handset and using FCA trading data to narrow suspects. Actionability is 3 on the generalisable-enforcement reading, because transaction reporting data quality is what made the case assemblable. Reach is 3, a sub-population of listed issuers, brokers and market surveillance functions.

**Action:** Treat this as a transaction reporting data quality argument for the next funding conversation: the evidential chain depended on reported data being complete enough to narrow a suspect set.

## On 12 November OFSI added an overseas business factsheet

**OFSI · UK · guidelines** · Sanctions Factsheet for Overseas Business, added to the UK financial sanctions guidance collection

Materiality: **Low** (1.8) · actionability 1/5 · reach 3/5

Published 2026-11-12

*Who it catches:* all regulated firms  
*Whose desk:* financial crime, compliance, legal

The UK financial sanctions guidance page's change log records one entry for 12 November 2026, "Added Sanctions Factsheet for Overseas Business", listed under general guidance. The factsheet, read on 2026-11-19, is headed "What are UK financial sanctions?" and carries no date. It restates existing obligations for UK persons and firms operating overseas: UK persons and companies are covered wherever they are, UK firms with overseas operations remain subject to UK obligations, and UK sanctions generally extend to the Crown Dependencies and Overseas Territories subject to each one's legislation; asset freezes cover entities a designated person owns or controls, so stakeholders and beneficial owners should be screened and not only the named party; UK, EU and US regimes are separate and compliance with one is not compliance with another; due diligence is not a one-time exercise; OFSI may license otherwise prohibited activity; and civil penalties apply on a strict liability basis, with criminal prosecution carrying up to seven years' imprisonment. Actionability is 1 because the factsheet is a restatement of existing obligations, which rubric.md scores 1; the pack scored it 2. Reach is 3, a sub-population of UK persons and firms with overseas operations. The record exists because a dated OFSI guidance addition belongs on the timeline and because an absence here would read as OFSI having published nothing in the window.

**Action:** Route the factsheet to whoever owns sanctions policy for overseas subsidiaries and branches, and check it against the group policy's statement of when UK sanctions apply; the addition changes no obligation.
